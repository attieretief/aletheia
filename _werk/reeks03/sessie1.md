# Wat is die Mens?

## Inleiding: 'n Nag by Sutherland

Sutherland in die Noord-Kaap is in die winter een van die koudste plekke in die land. Dit is ook een van die donkerste. Daarom staan die groot sterreteleskope daar op die koppie buite die dorp, die SALT-teleskoop die grootste van hulle, en daarom ry mense honderde kilometer om op 'n Julie-aand in die ryp te gaan staan en opkyk.

As jy dit al gedoen het, weet jy wat gebeur. Eers sien jy net sterre, meer as wat jy gedink het daar is. Dan begin jou oë gewoond raak, en die Melkweg kom te voorskyn soos 'n wolk wat oor die hele hemel gespan is. Laat in die nag wys iemand vir jou 'n vaag vlekkie laag in die noordooste: die Andromeda-sterrestelsel. Die lig wat nou in jou oog val, het daar vertrek lank voordat daar mense op aarde was.

En dan kom die gevoel. Jy is klein. Nie klein soos 'n kind in 'n groot huis nie, maar klein op 'n manier wat moeilik is om in woorde te sit. Hierdie hele ontsaglike ruimte gaan sy gang, en dit weet nie van jou nie.

Byna drieduisend jaar gelede het 'n herder in Israel dieselfde gevoel gehad en dit in 'n lied neergeskryf:

> As ek u hemel aanskou, die werk van u vingers, die maan en die sterre wat U daar geplaas het, wat is die mens dat U aan hom dink, die mensekind dat U na hom omsien? (Psalm 8:4–5)

*Wat is die mens?* Dit is die vraag van hierdie hele reeks. In hierdie eerste sessie gaan ons nog nie al die antwoorde gee nie. Ons wil eers die vraag self ernstig opneem. Ons kyk na die twee antwoorde wat ons kultuur gewoonlik gee, ons vra wat elkeen uitlaat, en ons gaan luister hoe Psalm 8 self die vraag hanteer. Aan die einde sal ons sien dat die psalmdigter iets raakgesien het wat ons maklik mis: hy kon net vra wat die mens is, omdat hy reeds geweet het wie God is.

## 1. Twee maniere om klein te voel

Die gevoel onder die sterre kan in twee rigtings gaan.

Die eerste rigting is wanhoop. In 1990 het die ruimtetuig Voyager 1, al ses miljard kilometer van die aarde af, op die sterrekundige Carl Sagan se versoek omgedraai en 'n foto teruggeneem. Die aarde is daarop skaars 'n pixel groot, 'n stippel in 'n straal sonlig. Sagan het die foto *Pale Blue Dot* genoem en geskryf dat al ons gewigtigheid, ons ingebeelde selfbelang en ons waan dat ons 'n bevoorregte plek in die heelal het, deur daardie flou liggie bevraagteken word. Hy het dit nie bedoel as 'n aanval op God nie. Hy wou ons nederig maak, en daarin het hy 'n punt. Maar die gevolgtrekking wat baie mense uit sulke foto's maak, is dat ons niks werd is nie. Groot heelal, klein mens, einde van die saak.

Die tweede rigting is verwondering, en die Franse wiskundige Blaise Pascal het dit in die sewentiende eeu beter as enigiemand onder woorde gebring. In sy *Pensées* skryf hy, vrylik vertaal:

> Die mens is maar 'n riet, die swakste ding in die natuur, maar hy is 'n denkende riet. Die hele heelal hoef nie op te staan om hom te verpletter nie; 'n damp, 'n druppel water is genoeg om hom dood te maak. Maar as die heelal hom sou verpletter, sou die mens nog edeler wees as dit wat hom doodmaak, want hy weet dat hy sterf, en hy weet watter voordeel die heelal oor hom het. Die heelal weet niks daarvan nie.

Kyk wat Pascal hier doen. Hy ontken nie die kleinheid nie. Hy sê selfs ons is kleiner as wat ons dink: 'n druppel water kan ons doodmaak. Maar hy wys op iets wat Sagan se foto nie kan wys nie. Die aarde is 'n stippel, ja. Maar op daardie stippel sit iemand wat die foto *verstaan*. Die heelal is groot, maar dit weet nie dat dit groot is nie. Jy weet dit.

Hier begin die vraag na die mens. Ons is klein, en ons weet dat ons klein is. Ons is deel van die natuur, en ons kan oor die natuur nadink. Wat se wese is dit?

## 2. Die eerste antwoord: "Niks anders as"

Die eerste moderne antwoord is eenvoudig en klink baie wetenskaplik. Die mens is niks anders as 'n besonder komplekse rangskikking van materie nie.

Francis Crick, wat saam met James Watson die struktuur van DNS ontdek het, het dit in 1994 so gestel:

> "Jy", jou vreugdes en jou smarte, jou herinneringe en jou ambisies, jou sin van persoonlike identiteit en vrye wil, is in werklikheid niks meer as die gedrag van 'n groot versameling senuselle en hulle gepaardgaande molekules nie.

Crick het sy boek *The Astonishing Hypothesis* genoem, die verbysterende hipotese. Twintig jaar vroeër het Richard Dawkins in *The Selfish Gene* geskryf dat ons oorlewingsmasjiene is, robotvoertuie wat blindelings geprogrammeer is om die selfsugtige molekules wat ons gene noem, te bewaar.

Ons moet eerlik wees oor wat hier waar is, want daar is baie. Die mens *is* van stof gemaak. Genesis 2:7 sê God het die mens uit die stof van die aarde gevorm, en Psalm 103:14 sê die Here weet hoe ons gemaak is, *Hy hou in gedagte dat ons stof is*. Die Bybel het nooit gedink die mens sweef bo die natuur nie. Ons liggame is uit dieselfde elemente gebou as die sterre, en ons is ten nouste verwant aan die res van die lewende wêreld. As 'n neuroloog vir jou wys hoe 'n beroerte 'n mens se persoonlikheid kan verander, is dit nie 'n bedreiging vir die geloof nie. Dit is 'n eerlike beskrywing van hoe God ons gemaak het.

Die probleem lê in twee klein woordjies: *niks anders as*. Die Skotse neurowetenskaplike Donald MacKay, self 'n gelowige, het hierdie denkpatroon spottend *nothing buttery* genoem, die "niks-anders-as-heid". Dit werk so. Jy beskryf iets korrek op een vlak, en dan sê jy dat daar geen ander vlak is nie. 'n Simfonie is lugtrillings. 'n Brief van jou ma is ink op papier. 'n Soen is spierbewegings. Elke keer is die eerste helfte waar en die tweede helfte 'n onbewese bewering. Die ink is werklik daar. Maar wie sê dat die brief niks anders as ink is nie, het nie verkeerd gemeet nie. Hy het vergeet om te lees.

En daar is 'n dieper probleem. Lees Crick se sin weer. Hy sê dat "jy", insluitend jou oortuigings, niks anders as senuselle is nie. Maar Crick self het daardie sin geskryf omdat hy oortuig was dat dit *waar* is, en hy wou hê jy moet dit glo omdat dit waar is. As sy oortuiging egter niks anders as die gedrag van molekules was nie, waarom sou ons dit vertrou? Molekules is nie waar of vals nie. Hulle beweeg net. Die "niks anders as"-antwoord laat altyd een ding uit: die persoon wat die antwoord gee. Ons het hierdie argument in [Reeks 2, Sessie 7]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks02-wetenskap/sessie7.html) volledig uitgewerk, en ons hoef dit nie hier te herhaal nie. Dit is genoeg om raak te sien dat die mens wat homself tot molekules wil terugbring, nog altyd daar staan en dink.

## 3. Die tweede antwoord: Die maatstaf van alles

Die tweede moderne antwoord loop presies die teenoorgestelde kant toe. Die mens is nie klein nie. Hy is die grootste ding wat daar is.

Ook dié antwoord is oud. Die Griekse denker Protagoras het in die vyfde eeu voor Christus gesê: *Die mens is die maatstaf van alle dinge.* Wat vir my waar is, is vir my waar. Wat vir jou waar is, is vir jou waar. (Plato bespreek die uitspraak in sy *Theaetetus*, en ons het in [die derde opstel van die Proloog]({{ site.baseurl }}/proloog/sessie3.html) gesien waarom dit nie as 'n teorie van waarheid kan staan nie.)

In die negentiende eeu het Friedrich Nietzsche die gedagte tot sy uiterste deurgevoer. As God dood is, het hy geredeneer, dan is daar niemand meer wat vir die mens kan sê wat goed en kwaad is nie. Dan moet die mens sy eie waardes skep. Nietzsche was baie eerliker as die meeste van sy navolgers: hy het geweet dat dit 'n ontsaglike, selfs skrikwekkende las is wat hy op die mens se skouers sit.

Vandag hoor ons hierdie antwoord meestal in 'n sagter vorm. Dit kom na ons in die taal van advertensies en selfhelpboeke: *Wees jouself. Volg jou hart. Niemand kan vir jou sê wie jy is nie.* Daar is ook hier iets wat waar is. Elke mens is uniek, en elke mens is verantwoordelik vir sy eie lewe. Die geloof sê nie jy moet jou persoonlikheid uitwis of jou gewete aan iemand anders oorgee nie. Die Reformasie is juis gebore toe 'n monnik voor die keiser gesê het dat hy nie teen sy gewete kan handel nie.

Maar die "maatstaf van alles"-antwoord het sy eie blinde kol. Dit maak die mens die bron van sy eie waarde, en dan kan hy dit ook self weer wegneem. As ek my eie waardes skep, kan ek ook besluit dat sommige mense minder werd is. Die twintigste eeu het gesien wat gebeur wanneer 'n mens, of 'n party, of 'n staat, homself die maatstaf maak. Pascal het dit al voorsien: *Die mens is nie 'n engel nie en ook nie 'n dier nie, en die ongeluk is dat hy wat die engel wil speel, die dier speel.*

## 4. Wat ons Grondwet aanneem

Hier is die eienaardige ding. Baie mense in ons tyd glo albei antwoorde tegelyk, en hulle merk nie die spanning op nie.

Neem die Suid-Afrikaanse Grondwet. Artikel 10 van die Handves van Regte sê: *Elkeen het ingebore waardigheid en die reg dat sy of haar waardigheid gerespekteer en beskerm word.* Dit is 'n pragtige sin, en dit is reg. Maar kyk na die woord *ingebore*. Die Grondwet sê nie die staat *gee* vir jou waardigheid nie. Dit sê jy het dit reeds, voordat enige wet geskryf is, net omdat jy 'n mens is. Die staat erken iets wat daar is.

Waar kom dit vandaan?

As die eerste antwoord reg is, en die mens is niks anders as molekules nie, is dit moeilik om te sê. Molekules het nie waardigheid nie. 'n Molekule in jou brein is nie meer werd as een in 'n klip nie. Ons kan natuurlik *besluit* om mense met waardigheid te behandel, maar dan is dit 'n ooreenkoms, nie 'n ingebore eienskap nie. En wat ons ooreengekom het, kan ons weer onderhandel.

As die tweede antwoord reg is, en die mens is die maatstaf van alles, is dit nie veel beter nie. Dan is my waardigheid so sterk as die mense wat besluit om dit te erken. Vir die swakkes, die siekes, die ongeborenes, die bejaardes en die wat niks het om te bied nie, is dit 'n dun beskerming.

Die idee dat elke mens 'n ingebore waardigheid het, is nie vanselfsprekend nie. Die antieke wêreld het dit nie geglo nie. In die Romeinse Ryk was dit gewoon om ongewenste babas, veral dogtertjies of kinders met gebreke, buite die stad te gaan neersit om te sterf. 'n Brief uit die tweede eeu, die *Brief aan Diognetus*, beskryf die Christene as mense wat soos almal anders trou en kinders kry, *maar hulle gooi nie hulle kinders weg nie*. Dit was opvallend genoeg om neer te skryf.

Waar het die Christene dit vandaan gekry? Uit die eerste hoofstuk van die Bybel: *God het die mens geskep as sy beeld* (Genesis 1:27). Ons gaan in [die volgende sessie]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks03-mens/sessie2.html) breedvoerig daaroor praat. Hier is dit genoeg om op te let dat ons Grondwet 'n aanname maak wat sy wortels in daardie teks het, of die skrywers dit bedoel het of nie. In Sessie 4 vra ons of 'n samelewing hierdie aanname kan behou as hy die wortel afkap.

## 5. Psalm 8: 'n Vraag in aanbidding

Kom ons gaan terug na die herder onder die sterre. Hy was nie die eerste of die laaste mens wat klein gevoel het nie. Maar kyk hoe hy die vraag vra.

Psalm 8 begin nie met die mens nie. Dit begin met God:

> Here, onse Here, hoe wonderlik is u Naam oor die hele aarde! (Psalm 8:2)

En dit eindig met presies dieselfde woorde (8:10). Die hele lied oor die mens staan tussen twee uitroepe oor God se Naam, soos 'n prent in 'n raam. Dit is nie toevallig nie. Die psalmdigter weet iets wat Sagan en Nietzsche op verskillende maniere vergeet het: die vraag *wat is die mens?* kan net beantwoord word binne die groter vraag *wie is God?*

Kyk ook na die woorde wat hy gebruik. Die Hebreeuse woord vir "mens" in vers 5 is *enosh*. Dit is die woord vir die mens in sy broosheid, die sterflike mens wat soos gras verdor. Die tweede uitdrukking, *ben-adam*, die "mensekind", herinner aan *adamah*, die grond waaruit hy gevorm is. Die psalmdigter vlei die mens nie. Hy noem hom by sy swakste naam.

En tog: *U dink aan hom. U sien na hom om.* Die werkwoord vir omsien, *paqad*, beteken om te besoek, om na iemand te gaan kyk, om sorg te dra. Dit is die woord wat gebruik word wanneer God na Sara omsien en sy 'n seun kry (Genesis 21:1). Die God wat die sterre met sy vingers gemaak het, kom kuier by die broos stofmens.

Dan gaan die psalm verder:

> Tog het U hom 'n bietjie minder as 'n goddelike wese gemaak, U het hom met eer en heerlikheid gekroon. U het hom heerskappy gegee oor die werk van u hande, alles het U aan hom onderwerp. (Psalm 8:6–7)

Die mens word gekroon. Hy kry 'n taak: om oor die skepping te heers, nie as 'n tiran nie, maar as God se onderkoning, iemand wat namens die Koning sorg. Die woorde herinner aan Genesis 1:26–28, waar die mens as God se beeld geskep word en dieselfde opdrag kry.

So hou Psalm 8 albei kante van die mens vas. Hy is *enosh*, broos en sterflik. En hy is gekroon, net 'n bietjie minder as 'n goddelike wese. Pascal het later dieselfde spanning beskryf as hy die mens 'n raaisel noem: regter van alle dinge en 'n swak erdwurm, die bewaarder van die waarheid en 'n riool van onsekerheid, die heerlikheid en die afval van die heelal.

Die verskil tussen Pascal se raaisel en die psalm se lofsang is waar die gewig lê. Vir die psalmdigter is die mens se grootheid nie iets wat hy self besit nie. Dit is iets wat God aan hom *gee*. Hy word gekroon; hy kroon homself nie. Daarom eindig die psalm nie met *hoe wonderlik is die mens* nie, maar met *hoe wonderlik is u Naam*. Die mens se waardigheid is 'n geskenk, en 'n geskenk wys altyd terug na die Gewer.

Dit is die antwoord wat nóg die eerste nóg die tweede moderne antwoord kan gee. Die eerste kan die mens se grootheid nie verklaar nie. Die tweede kan dit nie beveilig nie. Psalm 8 doen albei, omdat dit by God begin.

## 6. Dieselfde vraag in Job se mond

Ons moet eerlik wees. Psalm 8 se vraag word nie altyd in aanbidding gevra nie.

In die boek Job vra 'n man wat alles verloor het, wat sy kinders begrawe het en op 'n ashoop sit en krap aan sy sere, presies dieselfde vraag:

> Wat is 'n mens dat U hom so belangrik ag, dat U hom so dophou, dat U hom elke môre besoek, hom elke oomblik toets? (Job 7:17–18)

Dit is Psalm 8 binne-in uit gedraai. Vir die psalmdigter is dit genade dat God na die mens omsien. Vir Job voel dit soos 'n las. Hy wens God wil hom net uitlos.

Dit is goed dat hierdie verse in die Bybel staan. Die Skrif is nie bang vir die mens wat onder God se hand ly en nie verstaan nie. Hierdie reeks gaan nie die vraag oor lyding beantwoord nie; dit is die werk van Reeks 5. Maar dit is belangrik om reeds hier raak te sien dat Job se klag net sin maak omdat die mens is wat Psalm 8 sê hy is. 'n Klip kla nie oor God se aandag nie. Job kla, en sy klag is 'n soort getuienis: die mens is iemand by wie God betrokke is, al voel dit soms swaar.

## 7. Twee kennisse wat mekaar nodig het

Johannes Calvyn begin sy groot werk, die *Institusie van die Christelike Godsdiens*, met 'n sin wat amper soos 'n opsomming van Psalm 8 klink:

> Byna al ons wysheid, wat werklik as ware en betroubare wysheid kan geld, bestaan uit twee dele: die kennis van God en die kennis van onsself. (*Institusie* I.1.1)

Calvyn sê dan iets merkwaardigs. Hy erken dat dit nie maklik is om te sê watter een eerste kom nie. Ons kan nie na onsself kyk sonder om uiteindelik na God te kyk nie, want alles wat ons het, het ons van Hom ontvang. En wanneer ons ons eie armoede, swakheid en sonde raaksien, dryf dit ons na Hom toe. Maar andersom is dit ook waar: ons ken onsself nooit regtig nie *tensy* ons eers God se aangesig gesien het (I.1.2). Wie net na homself kyk, dink gou hy is regverdig, wys en sterk. Wie in God se lig staan, sien homself soos hy is.

Die kerkvader Augustinus het hierdie ontdekking op 'n baie persoonlike manier gemaak. In sy *Belydenisse* vertel hy hoe sy beste vriend in sy jeug skielik oorlede is. Die rou was so swaar dat alles om hom na die dood gelyk het. En dan skryf hy 'n kort sin in Latyn: *factus eram ipse mihi magna quaestio*. Ek het vir myself 'n groot vraag geword (*Belydenisse* IV.4.9).

Elkeen wat al gerou het, ken daardie ervaring. Skielik weet jy nie meer wie jy is nie. Augustinus het jare later die antwoord gevind, en hy het dit aan die begin van dieselfde boek geskryf: *U het ons vir Uself gemaak, en ons hart is onrustig totdat dit rus vind in U* (I.1.1). Die vraag wat hy vir homself geword het, kon net beantwoord word deur die Een wat hom gemaak het.

Dit verklaar ook waarom hierdie reeks ná Reeks 1 kom. In [Reeks 1]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks01-grondslag.html) het ons gevra wie God is: nie 'n wese onder ander wesens nie, maar die oneindige Grond van alle syn. In [Reeks 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks02-wetenskap.html) het ons gesien dat die fisiese wêreld nie in spanning met daardie God staan nie, maar sy werk is. Nou draai ons die vraag om. As dít is wie God is, en as dít die wêreld is wat Hy gemaak het, wie is ons daarin? Calvyn sou sê: ons kon nie hierdie vraag eerste vra nie. Ons moes eers opkyk.

## 8. "Maar ons sien Jesus"

Daar is nog een plek in die Bybel waar Psalm 8 aangehaal word, en dit verander alles.

Die skrywer van Hebreërs haal die psalm woordeliks aan: *Wat is die mens dat U aan hom dink?... U het alles aan hom onderwerp.* En dan maak hy 'n eerlike opmerking. Kyk rond, sê hy. *Ons sien nog nie dat alles aan hom onderwerp is nie* (Hebreërs 2:8). Die mens wat gekroon is om oor die skepping te heers, word deur siekte verslaan, deur sy eie sonde vasgevang en uiteindelik deur die dood ingehaal. Psalm 8 klink mooi, maar dit lyk nie of dit waar is nie.

*Maar ons sien Jesus*, gaan die skrywer voort, *wat vir 'n kort tydjie minder as die engele gemaak is, en wat ons nou sien met heerlikheid en eer gekroon omdat Hy die dood gely het* (2:9).

Hier kry Psalm 8 sy vervulling. Die Mens soos God die mens bedoel het, het werklik gekom. Hy het die broosheid van *enosh* aangeneem, tot in die dood. En Hy is gekroon. Die vraag *wat is die mens?* kry uiteindelik nie 'n definisie as antwoord nie, maar 'n Naam. Ons gaan in Sessie 8 terugkom hierheen, want dit is waar die hele reeks heen loop.

## Brug na Sessie 2

Psalm 8 sê die mens is gekroon, en Genesis 1 sê hoe: God het die mens geskep *as sy beeld*. Dit is waarskynlik die belangrikste stelling oor die mens wat ooit gemaak is. Dit het die idee van menswaardigheid in die Westerse wêreld ingedra, en dit lê agter elke woord in ons Grondwet se artikel 10.

Maar wat beteken dit? Is die beeld van God ons verstand, ons vermoë om lief te hê, ons gesag oor die aarde? Het die sondeval dit vernietig, of is dit nog daar? En wat beteken dit vir hoe ek my naaste behandel, ook die een wat ek nie verstaan of nie van hou nie? Dit is die vrae van [Sessie 2: Na God se Beeld]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks03-mens/sessie2.html).

## Bybelkommentaar oor Sleutelteksgedeeltes

**Psalm 8.** 'n Lofpsalm van Dawid, omraam deur dieselfde uitroep aan die begin en die einde (8:2, 10). Die raam is die boodskap: die mens word binne God se heerlikheid verstaan, nie daarbuite nie. Vers 3 is verrassend: God bou 'n vesting *uit die mond van kinders en suigelinge*, die swakste mense wat daar is. Die psalm se logika is dus van die begin af dat God sy krag in die kleines toon. Vers 6 se "goddelike wese" vertaal die Hebreeuse *elohim*, wat God, gode of hemelse wesens kan beteken. Die Griekse Septuagint het dit met "engele" vertaal, en Hebreërs 2:7 volg daardie vertaling. Die heerskappy van vers 7–9 sluit direk aan by Genesis 1:26–28: die mens is God se verteenwoordiger in die skepping, met die verantwoordelikheid om dit te versorg.

**Job 7:17–18.** Job neem Psalm 8 se vraag en keer dit om. Wat in die psalm genade is (God sien na die mens om), voel vir Job soos 'n vergrootglas waaronder hy ly. Die gebruik van dieselfde woordeskat (*enosh*, *paqad*) wys dat die skrywer bewus met die psalm in gesprek is. Die Bybel laat die klag staan; dit word nie weggeredeneer nie. God se antwoord kom eers in Job 38–42, en ons kom in Reeks 5 daarby uit.

**Hebreërs 2:5–9.** Die skrywer lees Psalm 8 as 'n belofte wat nog nie in die mensdom in die algemeen vervul is nie ("ons sien nog nie"), maar wel in Jesus ("maar ons sien Jesus"). Christus is die ware *ben-adam*, die Seun van die mens. Hy is "vir 'n kort tydjie" laer gemaak in die menswording en die lyding, en Hy is nou gekroon. Die gevolg in vers 10–11 is belangrik: Hy is nie skaam om ons broers te noem nie. Die heerlikheid wat Psalm 8 aan die mens beloof, kom na ons toe deur Hom.

## Besprekingsvrae

* **Onder die sterre:** Het jy al die gevoel van kleinheid gehad wat in die inleiding beskryf word? Het dit jou nader aan God laat voel, of verder? Waarom dink jy reageer mense so verskillend op dieselfde ervaring?

* **Niks anders as:** Kan jy voorbeelde dink uit die nuus, die skool of jou werk waar die mens as "niks anders as" iets beskryf word? Wat is waar in so 'n beskrywing, en wat word uitgelaat?

* **Die maatstaf:** "Wees jouself" en "volg jou hart" klink onskuldig. Waar sien jy dat hierdie idee mense help, en waar sien jy dat dit hulle alleen laat?

# What Is Man?

## Introduction: A night at Sutherland

In winter, Sutherland in the Northern Cape is one of the coldest places in the country. It is also one of the darkest. That is why the big telescopes stand on the hill outside the town, SALT the largest of them, and why people drive hundreds of kilometres to stand in the frost on a July night and look up.

If you have done it, you know what happens. At first you see only stars, more than you thought there were. Then your eyes adjust, and the Milky Way emerges like a cloud stretched across the whole sky. Late in the night someone points out a faint smudge low in the north-east: the Andromeda galaxy. The light entering your eye now left it long before there were people on earth.

And then the feeling comes. You are small. Not small like a child in a big house, but small in a way that is hard to put into words. This whole immense space goes about its business, and it does not know about you.

Nearly three thousand years ago a shepherd in Israel had the same feeling and wrote it down in a song:

> When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him? (Psalm 8:3–4, ESV)

*What is man?* That is the question of this whole series. In this first session we will not give all the answers yet. We first want to take the question itself seriously. We look at the two answers our culture usually gives, we ask what each leaves out, and we listen to how Psalm 8 itself handles the question. By the end we will see that the psalmist noticed something we easily miss: he could only ask what man is because he already knew who God is.

## 1. Two ways of feeling small

The feeling under the stars can go in two directions.

The first direction is despair. In 1990 the spacecraft Voyager 1, already six billion kilometres from earth, turned around at the request of the astronomer Carl Sagan and took a photograph back home. The earth in it is barely a pixel, a speck in a ray of sunlight. Sagan called the photograph *Pale Blue Dot* and wrote that our posturings, our imagined self-importance and our delusion that we have some privileged place in the universe are challenged by that faint point of light. He did not mean it as an attack on God. He wanted to make us humble, and in that he had a point. But the conclusion many people draw from such pictures is that we are worth nothing. Big universe, small human, end of story.

The second direction is wonder, and the French mathematician Blaise Pascal put it into words in the seventeenth century better than anyone. In his *Pensées* he writes, freely rendered:

> Man is only a reed, the weakest thing in nature, but he is a thinking reed. The whole universe need not rise up to crush him; a vapour, a drop of water is enough to kill him. But if the universe were to crush him, man would still be nobler than what kills him, because he knows that he dies, and he knows the advantage the universe has over him. The universe knows nothing of this.

Look at what Pascal does here. He does not deny the smallness. He even says we are smaller than we think: a drop of water can kill us. But he points to something Sagan's photograph cannot show. The earth is a speck, yes. But on that speck sits someone who *understands* the photograph. The universe is large, but it does not know that it is large. You do.

This is where the question of man begins. We are small, and we know that we are small. We are part of nature, and we can reflect on nature. What kind of creature is that?

## 2. The first answer: "Nothing but"

The first modern answer is simple and sounds very scientific. Man is nothing but a particularly complex arrangement of matter.

Francis Crick, who with James Watson discovered the structure of DNA, put it like this in 1994:

> "You," your joys and your sorrows, your memories and your ambitions, your sense of personal identity and free will, are in fact no more than the behavior of a vast assembly of nerve cells and their associated molecules.

Crick called his book *The Astonishing Hypothesis*. Twenty years earlier Richard Dawkins had written in *The Selfish Gene* that we are survival machines, robot vehicles blindly programmed to preserve the selfish molecules known as genes.

We should be honest about what is true here, because a great deal is. Man *is* made of dust. Genesis 2:7 says God formed man from the dust of the ground, and Psalm 103:14 says the Lord knows our frame, *he remembers that we are dust*. The Bible never thought man floats above nature. Our bodies are built from the same elements as the stars, and we are closely related to the rest of the living world. When a neurologist shows you how a stroke can change someone's personality, that is not a threat to faith. It is an honest description of how God made us.

The problem lies in two small words: *nothing but*. The Scottish neuroscientist Donald MacKay, himself a believer, mockingly called this pattern of thought *nothing buttery*. It works like this. You describe something correctly at one level, and then you say there is no other level. A symphony is vibrations in the air. A letter from your mother is ink on paper. A kiss is muscle movement. Each time the first half is true and the second half is an unproven claim. The ink really is there. But whoever says the letter is nothing but ink has not measured wrongly. He has forgotten to read.

And there is a deeper problem. Read Crick's sentence again. He says that "you", including your convictions, are nothing but nerve cells. But Crick wrote that sentence because he was convinced it was *true*, and he wanted you to believe it because it was true. Yet if his conviction was nothing but the behaviour of molecules, why should we trust it? Molecules are not true or false. They simply move. The "nothing but" answer always leaves out one thing: the person giving the answer. We worked this argument out fully in [Series 2, Session 7]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks02-wetenskap/sessie7.html), and we need not repeat it here. It is enough to notice that the man who would reduce himself to molecules is still standing there, thinking.

## 3. The second answer: The measure of all things

The second modern answer runs in exactly the opposite direction. Man is not small. He is the greatest thing there is.

This answer is old too. The Greek thinker Protagoras said in the fifth century before Christ: *Man is the measure of all things.* What is true for me is true for me. What is true for you is true for you. (Plato discusses the saying in his *Theaetetus*, and in [the third essay of the Prologue]({{ site.baseurl }}/proloog/sessie3.html) we saw why it cannot stand as a theory of truth.)

In the nineteenth century Friedrich Nietzsche carried the thought to its limit. If God is dead, he reasoned, then there is no one left to tell man what is good and evil. Then man must create his own values. Nietzsche was far more honest than most of his followers: he knew it was an immense, even terrifying burden that he was placing on human shoulders.

Today we mostly hear this answer in a softer form. It reaches us in the language of advertising and self-help books: *Be yourself. Follow your heart. No one can tell you who you are.* Here too something is true. Every person is unique, and every person is responsible for his own life. Faith does not say you must erase your personality or hand your conscience over to someone else. The Reformation was born precisely when a monk told the emperor that he could not act against his conscience.

But the "measure of all things" answer has its own blind spot. It makes man the source of his own worth, and then he can also take it away again. If I create my own values, I can also decide that some people are worth less. The twentieth century saw what happens when a man, or a party, or a state makes itself the measure. Pascal saw it coming: *Man is neither angel nor beast, and the misfortune is that whoever would play the angel plays the beast.*

## 4. What our Constitution assumes

Here is the strange thing. Many people today believe both answers at once, and they do not notice the tension.

Take the South African Constitution. Section 10 of the Bill of Rights says: *Everyone has inherent dignity and the right to have their dignity respected and protected.* It is a beautiful sentence, and it is right. But look at the word *inherent*. The Constitution does not say the state *gives* you dignity. It says you already have it, before any law was written, simply because you are human. The state recognises something that is there.

Where does it come from?

If the first answer is right, and man is nothing but molecules, it is hard to say. Molecules have no dignity. A molecule in your brain is worth no more than one in a stone. We can of course *decide* to treat people with dignity, but then it is an agreement, not an inherent property. And what we agreed, we can renegotiate.

If the second answer is right, and man is the measure of all things, it is not much better. Then my dignity is only as strong as the people who decide to recognise it. For the weak, the sick, the unborn, the elderly and those with nothing to offer, that is thin protection.

The idea that every human being has inherent dignity is not self-evident. The ancient world did not believe it. In the Roman Empire it was common to leave unwanted babies, especially girls or children with disabilities, outside the city to die. A second-century letter, the *Epistle to Diognetus*, describes Christians as people who marry and have children like everyone else, *but they do not cast away their offspring*. It was striking enough to write down.

Where did the Christians get it from? From the first chapter of the Bible: *God created man in his own image* (Genesis 1:27). We will speak about it at length in [the next session]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks03-mens/sessie2.html). Here it is enough to note that our Constitution makes an assumption with its roots in that text, whether its drafters intended it or not. In Session 4 we ask whether a society can keep this assumption once it cuts off the root.

## 5. Psalm 8: A question asked in worship

Let us go back to the shepherd under the stars. He was not the first or the last person to feel small. But look at how he asks the question.

Psalm 8 does not begin with man. It begins with God:

> O Lord, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth! (Psalm 8:1)

And it ends with exactly the same words (8:9). The whole song about man stands between two exclamations about God's name, like a picture in a frame. That is no accident. The psalmist knows something that Sagan and Nietzsche, in different ways, forgot: the question *what is man?* can only be answered inside the larger question *who is God?*

Look too at the words he uses. The Hebrew word for "man" in verse 4 is *enosh*. It is the word for man in his frailty, mortal man who withers like grass. The second phrase, *ben-adam*, "son of man", recalls *adamah*, the ground from which he was formed. The psalmist does not flatter man. He calls him by his weakest name.

And yet: *you are mindful of him. You care for him.* The verb for caring, *paqad*, means to visit, to go and look in on someone, to take care of them. It is the word used when God visits Sarah and she bears a son (Genesis 21:1). The God who made the stars with his fingers comes to call on frail, dusty man.

Then the psalm goes further:

> Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honour. You have given him dominion over the works of your hands; you have put all things under his feet. (Psalm 8:5–6, ESV)

Man is crowned. He receives a task: to rule over creation, not as a tyrant but as God's viceroy, someone who cares for things on the King's behalf. The words recall Genesis 1:26–28, where man is created as God's image and receives the same commission.

So Psalm 8 holds on to both sides of man. He is *enosh*, frail and mortal. And he is crowned, only a little lower than the heavenly beings. Pascal later described the same tension when he called man a riddle: judge of all things and a feeble earthworm, keeper of the truth and a sewer of uncertainty, the glory and the refuse of the universe.

The difference between Pascal's riddle and the psalm's praise lies in where the weight falls. For the psalmist, man's greatness is not something he possesses in himself. It is something God *gives* him. He is crowned; he does not crown himself. That is why the psalm does not end with *how majestic is man* but with *how majestic is your name*. Man's dignity is a gift, and a gift always points back to the Giver.

This is the answer neither the first nor the second modern answer can give. The first cannot explain man's greatness. The second cannot secure it. Psalm 8 does both, because it begins with God.

## 6. The same question in Job's mouth

We must be honest. Psalm 8's question is not always asked in worship.

In the book of Job a man who has lost everything, who has buried his children and sits on an ash heap scraping his sores, asks exactly the same question:

> What is man, that you make so much of him, and that you set your heart on him, visit him every morning and test him every moment? (Job 7:17–18, ESV)

This is Psalm 8 turned inside out. For the psalmist it is grace that God cares for man. For Job it feels like a burden. He wishes God would just leave him alone.

It is good that these verses are in the Bible. Scripture is not afraid of the person who suffers under God's hand and does not understand. This series will not answer the question of suffering; that is the work of Series 5. But it matters to notice already that Job's complaint only makes sense because man is what Psalm 8 says he is. A stone does not complain about God's attention. Job complains, and his complaint is a kind of testimony: man is someone God is involved with, even when it feels heavy.

## 7. Two knowledges that need each other

John Calvin opens his great work, the *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, with a sentence that sounds almost like a summary of Psalm 8:

> Nearly all the wisdom we possess, that is to say, true and sound wisdom, consists of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves. (*Institutes* I.1.1)

Calvin then says something remarkable. He admits it is not easy to say which comes first. We cannot look at ourselves without eventually looking at God, because everything we have we received from him. And when we see our own poverty, weakness and sin, it drives us towards him. But the reverse is also true: we never really know ourselves *unless* we have first looked on God's face (I.1.2). Whoever looks only at himself soon thinks he is righteous, wise and strong. Whoever stands in God's light sees himself as he is.

The church father Augustine made this discovery in a very personal way. In his *Confessions* he tells how his best friend died suddenly in his youth. The grief was so heavy that everything around him looked like death. And then he writes a short Latin sentence: *factus eram ipse mihi magna quaestio*. I had become a great question to myself (*Confessions* IV.4.9).

Anyone who has grieved knows that experience. Suddenly you no longer know who you are. Augustine found the answer years later, and he wrote it at the beginning of the same book: *You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you* (I.1.1). The question he had become to himself could only be answered by the One who made him.

This also explains why this series comes after Series 1. In [Series 1]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks01-grondslag.html) we asked who God is: not a being among other beings, but the infinite Ground of all being. In [Series 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks02-wetenskap.html) we saw that the physical world does not stand in tension with that God, but is his work. Now we turn the question around. If *that* is who God is, and if *that* is the world he made, who are we in it? Calvin would say: we could not have asked this question first. We had to look up.

## 8. "But we see Jesus"

There is one more place in the Bible where Psalm 8 is quoted, and it changes everything.

The writer to the Hebrews quotes the psalm word for word: *What is man, that you are mindful of him?... You have put everything in subjection under his feet.* And then he makes an honest observation. Look around, he says. *At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him* (Hebrews 2:8). Man, crowned to rule over creation, is defeated by illness, trapped by his own sin and finally overtaken by death. Psalm 8 sounds lovely, but it does not look true.

*But we see Jesus*, the writer goes on, *who for a little while was made lower than the angels, now crowned with glory and honour because of the suffering of death* (2:9).

Here Psalm 8 finds its fulfilment. The Human as God intended humanity to be has really come. He took on the frailty of *enosh*, all the way into death. And he is crowned. The question *what is man?* finally receives not a definition as its answer, but a Name. We will come back here in Session 8, because this is where the whole series is heading.

## Bridge to Session 2

Psalm 8 says man is crowned, and Genesis 1 says how: God created man *in his image*. It is probably the most important statement about man ever made. It carried the idea of human dignity into the Western world, and it lies behind every word of section 10 of our Constitution.

But what does it mean? Is the image of God our reason, our capacity to love, our authority over the earth? Did the fall destroy it, or is it still there? And what does it mean for how I treat my neighbour, including the one I do not understand or do not like? These are the questions of [Session 2: In the Image of God]({{ site.baseurl }}/reeks03-mens/sessie2.html).

## Bible Commentary on Key Passages

**Psalm 8.** A psalm of praise by David, framed by the same exclamation at the beginning and the end (8:1, 9). The frame is the message: man is understood within God's glory, not outside it. Verse 2 is surprising: God establishes a stronghold *out of the mouth of babies and infants*, the weakest people there are. From the start, then, the psalm's logic is that God shows his strength in the small. In verse 5, "heavenly beings" translates the Hebrew *elohim*, which can mean God, gods or heavenly beings. The Greek Septuagint rendered it "angels", and Hebrews 2:7 follows that translation. The dominion of verses 6–8 connects directly with Genesis 1:26–28: man is God's representative in creation, responsible for its care.

**Job 7:17–18.** Job takes Psalm 8's question and turns it upside down. What is grace in the psalm (God cares for man) feels to Job like a magnifying glass under which he suffers. The use of the same vocabulary (*enosh*, *paqad*) shows the writer is consciously in conversation with the psalm. The Bible lets the complaint stand; it is not reasoned away. God's answer comes only in Job 38–42, and we reach it in Series 5.

**Hebrews 2:5–9.** The writer reads Psalm 8 as a promise not yet fulfilled in humanity in general ("we do not yet see") but fulfilled in Jesus ("but we see Jesus"). Christ is the true *ben-adam*, the Son of Man. He was made lower "for a little while" in the incarnation and the suffering, and he is now crowned. The consequence in verses 10–11 matters: he is not ashamed to call us brothers. The glory Psalm 8 promises to man reaches us through him.

## Discussion Questions

* **Under the stars:** Have you ever had the feeling of smallness described in the introduction? Did it make you feel closer to God, or further away? Why do you think people respond so differently to the same experience?

* **Nothing but:** Can you think of examples from the news, school or your work where man is described as "nothing but" something? What is true in such a description, and what is left out?

* **The measure:** "Be yourself" and "follow your heart" sound innocent. Where do you see this idea helping people, and where do you see it leaving them alone?

* **Inherent dignity:** Our Constitution says every person has inherent dignity. How would you explain to a friend who does not believe where that dignity comes from? Can he explain it without God?

* **Job and the psalm:** The same question is asked with praise in Psalm 8 and with pain in Job 7. When in your life have you experienced God's attention as a comfort, and when as a burden?

* **For the group:** Calvin says we never truly know ourselves unless we have first seen God's face. What would it mean in practice to see yourself "in God's light"?
